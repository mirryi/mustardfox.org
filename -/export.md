In lines 78-80a of the “Dream of the Rood”:

::: {lang="ang"}
> Nu ðu miht gehyran, hæleð min se leofa,\
> þæt ic bealuwara weorc gebiden hæbbe,\
> sarra sorga.
:::

Roughly modernised, the Cross speaks:

> Now you may hear, my dear warrior,\
> that I have endured the works of wicked men,\
> grevious sorrows.

Here, the neuter acc. *[weorc]{lang="ang"}* is roughly Mdn.E. “work”, and *[bealuwara]{lang="ang"}*[^1] has been translated variously as “miscreants”, “wicked men”. Bosworth Toller’s gives “baleful inhabitants”, “criminals”.[^2] But apparently this bit has caused philologists some trouble.[^3]

As noted by Christopher Tolkien, one can compare also “Balrog”, which in Sindarin comes from *BAL* (“power”) + *raug/rog* (“demon”) but also seems to echo *[bealu-wearg]{lang="ang"}* (“outlaw”, “wolf”) or *[bealu-broga]{lang="ang"}* (“monster”, “terror”).[^4]

[^1]: This is perhaps the pl. gen. of an unrecorded *[\*bealuware]{lang="ang"}*, compounding *[bealu]{lang="ang"}* (“baleful”, “wicked”) and *[ware]{lang="ang"}* (“inhabitant”).

[^2]: Joseph Bosworth, ‘Bealu-Ware’, in *An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary Online*, ed. Thomas Northcote Toller, Christ Sean, and Ondřej Tichy, London: Oxford University Press; Prague: Faculty of Arts, Charles University, 2014. [[bosworthBealuware2014|q.v.]]

[^3]: G. C. Britton, ‘"Bealuwara Weorc" in the "Dream of the Rood"’, *Neuphilologische Mitteilungen* 68, no. 3 (1967): 273–76, <https://www.jstor.org/stable/43342353>. [[brittonBealuwaraWeorcDream1967|q.v.]]

[^4]: J. R. R. Tolkien and Christopher Tolkien, *The Shaping of [Middle-earth]{.nocase}: The Quenta, the Ambarkanta, and the Annals, Together with the Earliest ’Silmarillion’ and the First Map* (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1986). [[tolkienShapingMiddleearthQuenta1986|q.v.]]

title: on Life of the Beloved
date: 2025-07-31
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings, books, theology]
---
“Where is the gospel?” or so I found myself asking all throughout.[^1] In what is ostensibly an apologetic, Nouwen seems to shy away from any mention of what makes the Christian life at all distinctive and worthwhile: the redemptive power of Christ on the cross. One is taken and adopted as a child of God, through Christ’s work. One is blessed and broken, as Christ was blessed and broken. One is given for others, because Christ first gave himself. Here the Eucharist is used as a framing device yet entirely emptied of its meaning and power. For one is beloved *in* the Beloved. It’s no surprise, then, to find that his friends found the book uncompelling; how could it, without the gospel? What might have been a tender testament to the salvation that comes only from above becomes only impotent word-wrangling and hollow mysticism.

[^1]: Henri J. M. Nouwen, *Life of the Beloved* (New York: Crossroad, 1992). [[nouwenLifeBelovedSpiritual1992|q.v.]]

---
title: "pleading the fourth: where is thy warrant?"
date: 2026-02-21
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings, theology, philosophy, epistemology]
modified: 2026-08-06 17:19
---
Three detectives walk into a court. The first detective presents a piece of Knowledge collected from the defendant’s home. The Judge asks, “Did you have a warrant?” But he is a metaphysical naturalist, and he says, “No.”

“Then it is not admissible.”

The second detective presents a piece of Knowledge collected from the defendant’s home. The Judge asks, “Did you have a warrant?” But he is a pantheist, and he says, “No.”

“Then it is not admissible.”

Finally, the third detective presents a piece of Knowledge collected from the defendant’s home. The Judge asks, “Did you have a warrant?” He is a theist, and he says, “Yes, it was signed by Yourself.”

------------------------------------------------------------------------

It is clear and widely agreed that Knowledge requires Belief. For one cannot *know* something without in the first place possessing belief of its truth. It is ridiculous to say one knows the sky is blue when in fact he believes it is green. (Such a man is likely sick in body or mind.) But Belief alone is hardly sufficient to constitute Knowledge, for it is just as inappropriate to say that a man who in fact believes the sky is green therefore knows it is so. Indeed, another ingredient is the Truth of the matter. To know demands, then, belief in a true thing.

But is Knowledge *merely* the sum of Belief and Truth? It cannot be so: consider an ordinary man deep within an underground prison cell, where he is insulated from the whole outside world. There, he believes it is raining outside. Suppose it is indeed raining: even so, the insulated man, who cannot hear the rain nor feel its moisture, who receives neither news of the weather nor any other perception of the outside—does he *know* it is raining? In fact there are beliefs which are *warranted* and those which are not. But warranted beliefs cannot be formed in completely free fashion; indeed, common intuition tells us that genuine warrant must be formed in *right* or *appropriate* ways. For the man may believe it is raining outside due to the superstition that it always rains when he accidentally bites his tongue, yet such will not support the claim that he truly *knows* it is raining; his belief is hardly warranted. But if a man hears the pattering rain and see its droplets on the window, then his belief may well be well warranted. It is Appropriate Warrant which ties Belief of a True thing into Knowledge.

The precise nature of the notion of epistemic “appropriateness” governing proper and improper warrants is perhaps debated. (For instance, to avoid infinite regress, most contemporary epistemologists acknowledge that some beliefs—for instance, perceptual beliefs—must be *properly basic*, that is, warranted in themselves and not by virtue of having “reasons” for their truth.) In any case, even a cursory consideration reveals that notions of *epistemic warrant* are manifestly *normative*, that is, *prescriptive*: it pertains not to how beliefs *are* formed but how they *ought* to be formed.

There are severe implications, in the first place posing a severe problem for the place of Knowledge in metaphysical naturalism. For real Normativity itself finds no welcome in the home of the naturalist, to whom all phenomena are explicable in the terms of science, a *descriptive* discipline concerned only with what *is* but not what *ought to be*. Though the naturalist may grasp for ways to receive this desirable guest, none appear defensible. Ought is not reducible into Is. He cannot therefore accommodate epistemic warrant. Knowledge as such is an alien thing.

But, if one might for a moment imagine, what *would* it mean to reduce epistemic normativity to naturalistic terms? What about, for instance, a form of reliabilism based on statistical norms? A belief is warranted if it is formed by processes that give rise to predominantly true beliefs. Issues related to naturalistic explanations for belief aside, reliability is inadequate to constitute warrant as such reliably produced beliefs may be yet *merely accidental*. Or what if one supposed that natural laws give rise to epistemic warrant? Since all human thinking operates in strict accordance with natural laws, it must also operate in accordance with epistemic norms. Then all beliefs would warranted, even contradictory ones, inviting in all manner of irrationality.

What of \_super_naturalist views? It must welcome Knowledge where naturalism turns it away by accounting for genuine, objective Normativity. But even supposing Normativity as such is permitted, how might one understand epistemic normativity specifically? Two or three analyses seem possible.

A deontological perspective situates epistemic norms in terms of ethical ones, that is, Duty or Obligation. Ethical norms include epistemic norms: one knows something if he believes it and has not flouted obligations in believing it (such as ignoring relevant evidence against it). Or perhaps epistemic norms arise instead from teleological normativity, from, that is, Purpose or Design. In the framework of *proper function* as advocated by Plantinga and others, beliefs are warranted only if they formed by processes or systems (for instance, cognitive faculties) which are functioning properly. Of course, “proper” implies that the faculty is *intended* or *designed* to function in certain ways and not others. Finally, consider virtue epistemology, which seeks to remedy reliabilism by demanding, in addition to reliable processes, exercise of intellectual virtues such as honesty, thoroughness, and fairness.

It should be clear that none of these species of normativity have any place in thorough-going naturalism. (Anderson, which we summarise here, catalogs various objections, all of which are unsuccessful.[^1]) What of non-theistic Platonism, which holds to transcendent, abstract universals in addition to the immanent, concrete particulars of immediate reality? But it is difficult to see how the epistemic duty of deontological normativity, the cognitive design of teleological normativity, or the qualities of virtue normativity could arise from impersonal abstracta. Indeed, each of these is a thoroughly *personal* notion, exhibiting features such as intellect and volition; the grounds for epistemic normativity must be personal.

One is pushed towards the theistic. What if all is divine, as in a pantheistic view? The problem is immediate: there is therefore no distinction between that which imposes norms and that upon which norms are imposed, the *norms* and the *normed*. Indeed, the adherents of pantheism seem to suffer from the same problem as those who would reduce epistemic norms to natural law: if everything is divine, then everything is normative or normal. In fact, Knowledge demands a division between the *normative* and the *normal*. If one additionally holds to *monistic* pantheism—not only is all divine, all is One, and metaphysical distinctions and differentiations do not really exist—then he is even worse off: there is not even a difference between warranted and unwarranted beliefs, nor the subjects and objects of belief.

One might additionally consider panentheism, which dispenses the immutable and impassible divine of classical theism for a mutable and responsive mutualism: the universe is not identical to God (as in pantheism) but is a proper part of it. God consists of the immanent, non-divine universe (the ‘body’) and the transcendent, divine aspect (the ‘mind’ or ‘soul’). Neither is absolute or independent from the other, and it is their mutual dependency and interaction that accounts for God’s temporal change. The impersonal panentheist faces problems similar to the non-theistic Platonist: if God is not personal, then from where do epistemic duties or cognitive design arise? But even if God is personal, one must establish the specific grounds for epistemic norms. Ostensibly, the immanent would be subject to norms arising from the transcendent. This leads to several difficult questions. Firstly, would not the latter’s dependence on the former have ramifications on the nature of the norms? Indeed, panentheists are reluctant to ground obligations in the character or will of the divine, which is constantly changing in response to the immanent. And if the transcendent is privileged in this way, would it not make more sense to call it alone ‘God’?

Indeed, the briefest of reflections makes clear that it is the classically theistic view which comfortably and defensibly accounts for Knowledge proper. For epistemic norms find their absolute, objective source in the character of a personal, supernatural God and his creation of human beings to reflect his image. It is natural that creatures should possess duties and obligations toward their Creator. Notions of proper function are perfectly sensible because God created humans to exercise rational cognitive and perceptive faculties. And epistemic virtues may reasonably reflect qualities valued by God. It is thanks to the sharp distinction between Creator and creature that the norm and the normed can exist in consistent and Knowledge-enabling relationship.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Then deny Him if thou wilt! But do not then say that thou knowst that God doth not exist, or that He is impersonal or universal or dependent on us; for where is thy warrant? Therefore we speak: if Knowledge, then God.

[^1]: James N. Anderson, *The Theistic Preconditions of Knowledge: A Thumbnail Sketch*, https://www.proginosko.com/docs/knowledge_and_theism.html, 2010. [[andersonTheisticPreconditionsKnowledge2010|q.v.]]

In a thoroughly engaging biography of Luther, Bainton plants the reformer firmly in his own political and theological landscape: the final decades of the medieval age, and the age of the Renaissance and of nationalism.[^1] He traces Luther’s thought by way of the man’s own writings and those of his allies, his observers, and his enemies. Ample discussion of the personal and theological contemporaries supplements without distracting too much from Luther himself. The picture is complex; the man is variously polemic and deferential, sometimes unreservedly jocular, so much that occasional glimpses at his tenderness are startling. It is one of a titan of church history: a passionate theologian and a pastor, a musician and a prolific scholar, a father; above all, an intensely and painfully devotional Christian.

title: board game algebrae
date: 2026-03-29
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings]
---
Usually, card games are one-dimensional. I don’t mean that they’re shallow and boring, but I mean it in a quite literal sense. There is one dimension, and it is usually the vertical one, or, perhaps more accurately, the temporal one. The cards are drawn from stacks and placed in piles. Of course, most games are not *strictly* one dimensional—the “dimensions” of *hands* and other common mechanisms seem hard to pin down—but at least predominantly so. These games center around the_order\_ in which cards are drawn and played.

It was a delight recently to play card-based games with additional, non-temporal, spatial dimensions. For instance, *Innovation*,[^1] in which players compete to build civilisations across the history of technological innovation, has each player develop stacks of cards based on type. It is mainly the top card of each stack which matters, but under certain circumstances a stack may be *splayed*, or spread out to reveal and activate parts of the cards below. This mechanism is directional: splaying *right* vs. *left* vs. *diagonally* reveals different parts of each card. This flavour of spatial dimensionality is not uncommon: *Arboretum*[^2] and *Forest Shuffle*[^3] likewise exploit the rectangular geometry of cards. In the former, tree cards are arranged in a grid to score points; in the latter, animal cards are situated on the four sides of the tree cards. In *Wingspan*,[^4] bird cards are arranged in habitat rows, affecting the order of effect activation, and certain birds can move between rows.

Each of these games might be rightly called tableau-builders, that is, games in which players develop and manipulate an array, or tableau, of components, often tiles. From this perspective, what’s happening becomes obvious: cards are playing a double role. They are cards, as in the traditional sense, to be drawn and held and discarded; but they are also tiles, to be placed and rearranged. The rectangular card is a familiar, canonical form, and they readily serve as rectangular tiles. But, in principle, tiles may be of hexagonal, circular, or other non-rectangular shapes. Bird cards could be bird-shaped. Perhaps this raises certain mathematical questions of interest to few if any. If we strip away all accidental features, what is the fundamental, algebraic structure of each of these games? Can these informal intuitions about “dimensionality” be precisely characterised? Can informal categories of “game mechanics” be “verified” by mathematical structure? Or we should just stop ruining fun.

[^1]: Chudyk, Carl, *Innovation* (Asmadi Games, 2010). [[chudykcarlInnovation2010|q.v.]]

[^2]: Cassar, Dan, *Arboretum* (Renegade Game Studios, 2018). [[cassardanArboretum2018|q.v.]]

[^3]: Kosch, *Forest Shuffle* (Lookout Games, 2023). [[koschForestShuffle2023|q.v.]]

[^4]: Hargave, Elizabeth, *Wingspan* (St. Louis, MO: Stonemaier Games, 2019). [[hargaveelizabethWingspan2019|q.v.]]

title: components of the self-authenticating canonical model
tags: [musings, theology, philosophy, epistemology]
modified: 2026-08-24 11:29

date: 2026-08-17
author: Eric Zhao
---
*Canon Revisited* argues that “God has created the proper epistemic environment wherein belief in the New Testament canon can be reliably formed.”[^1] Recall that [[vgm5|the question at hand]] is the *de jure* objection: even supposing that the New Testament canon is true, how can Christians *know* that it is true? Truth does not necessarily confer epistemic access in general. But in this case, Scripture itself testifies to this epistemic environment. Unlike the [[zc6q|externally authenticated models]], which cannot authenticate the canon as the ultimate rule over the church, the *self-authenticating canonical model* properly grounds the Scripture, which bears the authority of God, who “\[swears\] by himself,” in itself as the ultimate authority.[^2]

In this epistemic environment, it is as Christ says: “My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me.”[^3] Christ’s voice is heard, and it is recognised as such by those whom he knows. For it bears the qualities of His voice, and those who know Christ and are known by him recognise it, just as a child recognises the call of his father or mother amidst a crowd of voices. So it is with Scripture which, being God’s Word, bears *attributes of canonicity*, not least among these divine qualities, or “marks” of divine origin. These attributes are recognised by his people whom he knows; the internal testimony of the Holy Spirit overcomes sin’s noetic effects to produce belief that the books are from God.

But Christ’s voice must first be heard at all, or else it cannot be followed. In the same way, the church cannot cannot recognise as canonical a book that it does not have. The church as a whole must be *exposed* to the book. This is quite critical; unless God *providentially exposed* the book to the church, then the church has no basis for supposing that it possesses a *complete* canon. Fortunately, Scripture attests to God’s intent in giving his Word to his people. The pattern of God’s redemptive-historical activity is to redeem a covenant community and then “*give them covenantal documents* that testify to that redemption.”[^4] And he is, of course, quite able to accomplish his intent. We can know, then, that the church at least knows of all the books which are canonical.

The canonical model must still, from the books available, distinguish the canonical from the non-canonical. The former books are distinguished by *attributes of canonicity*, for which the Scripture provides. In the first place, it is quite reasonable to expect that the Word of God should bear marks of divinity. John Murray reasons: “If the heavens declare the glory of God and therefore bear witness to their divine creator, the Scripture as God’s handiwork must also bear the imprints of his authorship.”[^5] These marks can be found in Scripture’s beauty, its efficacy, and its harmony, as say the Westminster Divines: “the heavenliness of the matter, the efficacy of the doctrine, the majesty of the style, the consent of all the parts, the scope of the whole (which is, to give all glory to God), the full discovery it makes of the only way of man’s salvation, the many other incomparable excellencies, and the entire perfection thereof, are arguments whereby it doth abundantly evidence itself to be the Word of God.”[^6]

Granted, however, that such marks are objectively present, how can one recognise and know that they are there? Moreover, many people do not acknowledge such marks exist nor, of course, accept Scripture as revelation from God. In fact, Scripture itself readily anticipates this: there are persons who do not “accept the things of the Spirit of God.”[^7] But those who do accept them do so thanks to the indwelling of the same Spirit. The Apostle John declares that the Spirit will “guide you into all truth,” and Paul likewise writes that “we have received not the spirit of the world, but the Spirit who is from God, that we might understand the things freely given us by God.”[^8] The Spirit overcomes the noetic effects of sin to produce belief in the Scriptures.

This *testimonium* of the Spirit is active corporately and covenantally, just as God’s redemptive activity is covenantal. We should expect God’s covenantal documents to be received at a covenantal level. In this way, the reception of the New Testament books by the church is in fact an indicator of their canonicity. Indeed, the church’s reception is “a natural and inevitable outworking of the self-authenticating nature of Scripture.”[^9] The self-authenticating view means furthermore that, as Bavinck writes, “the testimony of the Holy Spirit is not a private opinion but the witness of the church of all ages.”[^10] Of course, this does not imply absolute unity amongst the church, but one should expect—and indeed, one finds—*predominant* unity with respect to the composition of the canon.

God’s redemptive work is furthermore *historical*, and the New Testament books are the “authoritative apostolic interpretation” of that “particular historical epoch in which God has acted in Jesus Christ.”[^11] For Christ entrusted his message to his apostles, saying, “The one who hears you hears me, and the one who rejects you rejects me, and the one who rejects me rejects him who sent me.”[^12] The pattern of Scripture, indeed, attests to the entrusting of God’s covenant documents to his covenant community *through* certain key messengers. Just as Moses and the prophets spoke with the authority of God to transmit the Scriptures of the Old Testament, the apostles “presided over the transmission of the apostolic deposit and labored to make sure the message of Christ was firmly and accurately preserved for future generations, through the help of the Holy Spirit.”[^13] Hence the New Testament is “a collection of apostolic writings—writings that bear the authoritative message of the apostles and derive from the foundational apostolic era (even if not directly from their hands).”[^14] Inasmuch as the New Testament books bear marks of divine origin, they bear evidence of *apostolic origin*, and here historical evidences as warranted by Scripture itself serve to testify to their canonicity.

This is the self-authenticating canonical model: the church is *providentially exposed* to the Scripture, which bears *attributes of canonicity*—its *divine qualities*, *corporate reception*, and *apostolic origins*—rightly recognised through the *internal testimony of the Holy Spirit*.

---
Having recently [read too much about Orpheus](ff4x), I find myself looking for something resembling an Orphic katabasis in other stories… The Labyrinth in *The Tombs of Atuan* seems awfully underworld-ish.[^1]

Ged’s to-be-legendary heist is to reclaim that half of Ring of Erreth-Akbe which is kept deep under the Temple of the Nameless Ones on the Kargish isle of Atuan. He succeeds. But arguably, there’s something much greater that he reclaims from the dark: a human soul, Tenar’s. I don’t suppose he expected do so, but I can’t help but see some Orpheus-like elements to his descent and return from the darkness.

He’s no artist of music, but an artist of names, a mage, and indeed perhaps the greatest, at least since Erreth-Akbe. Into the darkness he goes with his instrument of power; he contends with the Nameless Hades therein, his art against their heaving will, that he wards off a great earthquake. The Arha he finds and leads back up (or rather she with her knowledge of the winding tunnels leads him up) is but a mere shadow of who she could be. But he is the [[ff4x|successful Orpheus]], for together they burst forth into the light of day, and Tenar is, in all ways but that of flesh, reborn.

Then one is reminded of the Sir Orfeo, for they fare from that dark Place, climbing forest and peak out of the desert, and return to the isles of the Archipelago. And indeed, after many years, a throne is to be reclaimed (though not quite by Ged).

title: on Lay Me in God's Good Earth
date: 2025-12-30
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings, books, theology]
---
In his treatment of the body one confesses his anthropogeny and eschatology.[^1] He who sees only the blackness of the void looks anxiously to its preservation in perpetuity, that he might cheat death by any instrument available. He who scorns it as a mere vat for the soul—now perhaps a necessary apparatus but later a worthless husk—allows it to languish, or mortifies it beyond recognition. Incineration, then, is a fitting end. But he who recognises its God-given worth accords it due honor and stewards it in health whilst resting his ultimate hope elsewhere: in the Maker who creates and *recreates*.

It is a distinctively biblical eschatology which allows followers of Christ to engage with death forthright, not shrinking away in fear; with proper mourning yet in surpassing joy; recalling always our sure hope in the “Maker of heaven and earth” who brings about “the communion of saints, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body, and the life everlasting.” For those whose trust lies in God can look to their creation in His image.[^2] And they can look to the body of the risen Christ to perceive what awaits them: that which is sown in weakness and dishonor shall be raised in power and glory; that which is natural and perishable shall be spiritual and imperishable; that which is earthly shall be heavenly. The man of dust dies, but the man of heaven lives, and we who have borne the image of the man of dust shall also bear the image of the man of heaven.[^3]

Natural burial, then, as the authors argue, is a fitting confession of the Christian hope. The body is left unembalmed, for such invasive efforts to forestall decomposition, far from preserving the body, do violence to it. Neither are they suitable nor even possible, for the body is sown perishable and resown imperishable. It is set to decompose alongside its covering or its casket in the ground without a vault, affirming that God raised us out of dust and will recreate us from dust. Cremation, on the other hand, does deliberate violence to the beauty of the divine creation; one must ask if it honors the dead as children of God whose bodies will be resurrected on the last day.

There is more to reclaim, however, than burial alone. In a culture in which the affairs of death have been relinquished to secular professionals, even Christians have fallen upon the impulse to sanitize death of its discomfort. Whilst living, death, though superficially acknowledged, is something taboo, hardly to be spoken of in seriousness, even among those at its door. It is to be staved off at all costs, and the dying perish in hospitals, nursing homes, and funeral palours, suffering aggressive treatments among unfamiliar faces and sterile equipment. Once dead, their bodies are impure and unclean, to be handled by undertakers, away from the eyes of the living. They are “cleansed” through embalming as though decay and the realities of death might be warded off. They are buried in manicured cemeteries far “out there,” away from sight, with cement vaults and liners to “protect” them from the earth. Among the living, grief is made a private affair, and orchestrated by funeral dramaturgists who move mourners through a production by which emotions are released and comfort found.

What can the church offer in a culture whose relationship with death has been so confused? It can speak of death with severity yet hope, affirming its sorrow whilst rejecting its finality and allaying its fear; Christ has passed through death before us, who tells us, “Do not be afraid”.[^4] It can accompany the body unto its burial place, to care for and watch over it, to transport it and inter it into the ground; and in doing so confess the body not as a foul carcass but an honorable vessel of God. It can comfort the living in word and presence; for to live is Christ, and to die is to gain Christ.[^5] The church offers, in other words, nothing short of the gospel of Jesus Christ. The process of death is an opportunity to bear witness to the power of that gospel; the gospel which teaches us to face death—and thus life—with a right seriousness and joy.

[^2]: Gen. 1:27.

[^3]: 1 Cor. 15:35–49.

[^4]: Matt 28:10.

[^5]: Phil. 1:21.

---
title: "\"Gnosis and Time\""
date: 2026-02-01
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings, theology]
---
Hellenistic thinkers appear to have conceived of reality in degrees of inferiority, in which a divine permanence and perpetuity occupies the summit of the hierarchy.[^1] The movement and change perceived in Time, then, is cyclical, for the identical recurrence of the same things—thus ensuring their survival—is the natural expression of that ultimate reality. The immediate reality is governed by its superior realities of increasing regularity in the cosmic order, the celestial bodies and luminaries, who are empowered by if not of divine natures. The consequences are immediate and severe: notions of past and present break down, because no event is unique but has occurred, occurs, and will occur again, eternally. There is neither beginning nor end, nor can one fathom any kind of continuous progress in between. Cities rise, corrupt, fall, and rise again. Troy was before yet after, and therefore always. *History* does not exist as such.

This cyclical and ultimately atemporal conception of reality is at obvious odds with Christian understanding. For those in Christ, indeed, Time is rectilinear and continuous, having a definite starting point at the creation of the universe and an equally definite ending point at the impending Parousia. In between them is the Incarnation and the Resurrection. The former is definitively before the later, and both are concrete, irreplaceable, and *unique*: the Son of God took on humanity, died, and rose again *once* for all (Heb. 10:10, 1 Pet. 3:18). Whereas continuous progression is unthinkable in a Greek cosmos, divine Providence governs and orients the development of history, which with each step draws irreversibly nearer to its good and singular end.

The Gnostic philosophers embraced not some middling compromise between these diametrically opposed two but an autonomous conception bearing similarities to either whilst rejecting both. In this respect one can see that Gnostic teachings are neither mere Hellenisations of orthodox doctrine nor the result of Christian influences on Greek cosmology. Indeed, the various strains of Gnosis (such as Manichaeism, Hermeticism, or Mandaeism) testify to its emergence and development in non-Christian, pre-Christian, and anti-Christian flavours, in any case exceeding the dimensions of outright Christian heresy.

Naturally, the Gnostic’s conception of Time is caught up in his understanding of Salvation. Oppressed by the utterly scandalous presence of evil, he understands that he must by nature be or have originally been something other than and outside the Prison of alien servitude in which he finds himself. He is homesick for some transcendent reality, a former yet not completely forgotten paradise. This he shares with the Christian. But where the Christian understands that the creation, though created good by almighty God, has fallen by the sin of man, the Gnostic finds the creation itself wicked, a trap of enslavement fashioned by a lesser Creator. This *demiurge* is unlike the wise and good artisan of Platonic philosophy; he is foolish and evil. Where the Greek firmament evoked the awe and admiration of its observers, for the Gnostic the whole cosmos seems a deep and horrible tragedy. One Mandaean laments, “In this world \[of Darkness\] I lived for a thousand myriad years, and no one knew that I was there…. The years followed the years, the generations followed the generations: here I was and they did not know that I was living here, in their world.” For Marcion, the body is a “sack of excrement,” and, for the Archons, “being ignorant… \[the demiurge\] took counsel with his Powers; they engendered Destiny and bound the gods of the heavens, the angels, the demons, and men in measures and times \[*chronos*\] and moments \[*kairos*\].” Irenaeus, writing of the Marcosians, records that “they declare that the Demiurge, desiring to imitate the infinitude, and eternity, and immensity, and freedom from all measurement by time of the Ogdoad \[being the eighth and final heaven\] above, but, as he was the fruit of defect, being unable to express its permanence and eternity, had recourse to the expedient of spreading out its eternity into times, and seasons, and vast numbers of years, imagining, that by the multitude of such times he might imitate its immensity. They declare further, that the truth having escaped him, he followed that which was false, and that, for this reason, when the times are fulfilled, his work shall perish.”

Time, then, whether a product of ignorance or of malice, is a false imitation, a caricature of an immeasurably superior reality. There is need for a Salvation to deliver man from temporal suffering. The solution is found in the act of *gnosis*, or *epignosis*, that is, the “recognition” that the present humiliation is accidental and temporary, that he yet retains his true nature separate from base flesh. It is this reawakening that features across all strains of Gnostic thought: to regain knowledge of oneself is to redeem onself. The soul, or spirit, which is the true self, is detached from the prison of the body, fully restoring him to his atemporal condition. It is a regeneration, and a liberation from the tyranny of the cosmos into positive freedom, often the license to do whatever he pleases, even the most seemingly lecherous of acts. The Gnostic is utterly saved by nature and not by works; amoralism seems to some a fitting way to demonstrate his contempt for the material enterprise. Whether Time is a circle patterning immobility or a singular line, it is to be broken and destroyed.

This soteriological mechanism appears to be largely atemporal in nature. For “pneumatic” or “spiritual” men, the revelation is sudden and is in no way contingent on past events. Of course, it cannot be said that all temporal elements are excised. Valentinianism, for instance, derives the content of its saving revelation from claims to a secret tradition transmitted over the course of history. Other systems profess a succession of prophets including the oldest Biblical patriarchs or other figures such as Zoroaster, the Buddha, or Jesus. Even so, the person is typically expunged of all historical content, distilled for its transcendent meaning. The docetics assert that the Christ was not really a man but purely spiritual. His body, his sufferings, the Crucifixion, and the Resurrection are all emptied of concrete and temporal reality; it is the intellectual, atemporal, or *mythical*, *example* that is essential. In this way, the flow of history is seen not in concrete and causal terms but through a mythical lens by which particular events reflect a supercosmic drama. Then time, if it must be ascribed a geometry, is to the Gnostic like a series of broken line segments: “It is the diverse phases of this mythical drama that give to history whatever meaning it can have, or rather that interrupt the course of concrete time, chopping it into an incoherent succession of dramatic episodes: sudden interventions of the transcendent in human affairs, sudden acts of God, which nothing justifies and nothing prepares the way for, arbitrary irruptions which put the finishing touches to the inorganic picture of Gnostic time.”[^2]

[^1]: [Henri-Charles Puech et al.]{.nocase}, ‘Gnosis and Time’, in *Papers from the Eranos Yearbooks, Eranos 3*, Man and Time (Princeton University Press, 1957), 38–84, <https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt183pqp0.8>. [[puechGnosisTime1957|q.v.]]

[^2]: [Puech et al.]{.nocase}, 83. [[puechGnosisTime1957|q.v.]]

---
title: on the original Orpheus
date: 2024-08-09
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings, arda]
modified: 2026-08-06 18:02
---
The tale of Orpheus and Eurydice as now popularly known ends in tragedy. The bereaved Orpheus descends into the underworld, harp or lyre in hand; there, he plays music so sweet as to move Charon, and Cerberus, and even Hades, that the king of the underworld grants him to take Eurydice back into the realm of the living, with the condition that he must not look backwards whilst they’re still in the nether’s darkness. In some versions, as in Virgil’s *Gregorics* (first century BCE), he forgets this charge; approaching the light of the living world, the joy-enraptured Orpheus looks to share his delight with Eurydice—and she’s taken away from him. Other accounts, as in Ovid’s *Metamorphoses X* (first century CE), suggest that he’s possessed by a fear that the gods have tricked him. Either way, the famous musician fails and returns alone, twice-robbed.

Later, it seems that medieval authors began to conflate the character of Orpheus with the figure of Christ, perhaps in a reinterpretation of the katabasis as the Harrowing of Hell. A fourteenth century French author writes of the tale in *Ovide Moralisé* that “by Orpheus and by his harp we must understand the person of our Lord Jesus Christ… who played his harp so melodiously that he drew from Hell the sainted souls of the Holy Fathers who had descended there because of the sins of Adam and Eve.”[^1] We are, as a result, left with works such as the much less well-known Middle English Breton lai “Sir Orfeo”, itself perhaps based on French works. But there’s a crucial transformation of the core arc known from antiquity: Sir Orfeo *succeeds* in retrieving his wife Heurodis from the fairy king and reclaims his throne in Winchester. It would seem that Christian influences made the original catastrophe instead eucatastrophic.

Yet what if this wasn’t a novel alteration, but more like a return to the true original?

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Apparently, plenty of scholars of antiquity have argued that Orpheus originally *succeeded* in leading Eurydice back to the land of the living, to the extent that John Heath observes in a 1994 essay:

> The nearly universal conclusion of twentieth-century scholarship has been that the earliest and most pervasive pre-Virgilian account of Orpheus’ catabasis related his unqualified success: the bard charms the powers of the underworld into releasing his wife, and she returns with him to the land of the living. The more familiar tale of a second loss due to the breaking of a tabu, according to the prevailing opinion, is the invention of the Hellenistic world.[^2]

Curiously, very little about such a lost comedy can be found by searching online, outside of scholarly sources. Neither [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orpheus_and_Eurydice) nor [Encyclopedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Orpheus-Greek-mythology) seem to make any mention of the possibility. I’m left wondering why this is so, if indeed that “Orpheus’ ‘complete victory’ has found its way into the mythological handbooks, introductory texts, and commentaries.”[^3]

I was introduced to such notions some weeks ago by a paper from Giovanni Costabile, “Orpheus and the Harrowing of Hell in the Tale of Beren and Lúthien”, which discusses some fascinating connections between the tale of Orpheus’s katabasis, Sir Orfeo, Christ, the Harrowing of Hell, and Tolkien’s cherished Tale of Beren and Lúthien, and wherein he summarizes this argument that the earliest clear account of the Orpheus and Eurydice had a happy ending.[^4]

Such a debate exists, of course, because no manuscripts of such an original are left to us; what we *are* left consists of a number of allusions by pre-Virgillian authors which, the critics claim, strongly suggest Orpheus’s success. I’ll summarize a few of the references here; folks interested in the rest can refer to the full paper, Heath’s article, and the thesis of Kyle Merkley.[^5]

It seems that the earliest allusion cited by scholars is from *Alcestis*, a fifth century BCE tragedy by Euripides. In Admetus’s lamentation over Alcestis, who willing sacrificed her life for him, he bemoans:[^6]

> If I had the words and music of Orpheus to charm Persephone or her spouse with my songs and take you from Hades, I would have made the descent. Then neither Pluto’s hound nor soul—conduction Charon at his oar would have stopped me until I set you alive once more in the light of day.

The argument is thus: these lines wouldn’t make much sense—be absurd, even—if Orpheus was unsuccessful. Next, in the fourth century, Plato writing as Phaedrus in *Symposium*, declares:

> But Orpheus, the son of Oeagrus, the gods sent back from Hades a failure. They showed him a mere phantom of the wife for whom he made the journey. They did not given him the real thing because he seemed to be a coward, seeing that he was a harp-player and did not have the heart to die for his love as Alcestis had. Instead he schemed to enter Hades while still alive, and so for this reason the gods punished him and arranged for his death to come at the hands of women.[^7]

Phaedrus praises Alcestis’s self-sacrifice in love, so powerful is true love that leads to noble deeds. Orpheus, in contrast, is punished by the gods for his weakness, his failure to demonstrate true love: instead of dying for his wife, he tries to retrieve her from the underworld whilst still alive; in response, the gods supply with him with a mere ghost. But critics assert that nevertheless Orpheus is clearly portrayed as succeeding and that Plato, in order to serve his narrative, is merely warping the returned Eurydice into a ghost.

Also from the fourth century, the elegiac Hermesianax writes that “with song he won the underworld’s great lords, for Agriope to regain the gentle breath of life.”[^8] Here, Orpheus’s wife is named for the first time, not as Eurydice but as Agriope. But anyway, scholars argue that this clearly suggests that the musician was successful. The evidence mounts: a number of ancient sources (the aforementioned, and *Busiris*, *Lament for Bion*, and Diodorus) allude to an Orpheus who seems to achieved the light of day with the resurrected Eurydice.

Not everyone agrees. Heath’s 1994 article, indeed, seeks to refute each of the cited examples and asserts that there is no unequivocal evidence for a pre-Christian successful Orpheus.[^9] He argues that the supporters of the happy ending have elected for overly optimistic interpretations of each reference and taken them out of context. In his lament, for example, Admetus refers to Orpheus’s enchanting of Hades—and all agree that this was an unequivocal success, for Hades returned Eurydice to him *in the underworld*—but not that he actually brought her out. Furthermore, in the midst of all the hysterical and wildly inappropriate things pronounced by Admetus in this lament,[^10] one can only take his statements in irony. It makes more sense to understand his allusion to Orpheus as the utterings of a self-absorbed man caught up in his own grief; he wishes he had Orpheus’s powers, but that the legendary bard in fact failed is completely lost on him—but not on the audience. And in the context of the entire play, Heath argues that “it is \[Orpheus’s\] traditional inefficacy that fits closely with the major themes.”[^11]

The opposition to the interpretation of Phaedrus’s statements is similar: indeed the gods presented Orpheus with his wife in the underworld, but nothing is said about a successful return. And if Orpheus did succeed, wouldn’t this subvert Phaedrus’s point, which anyway is mainly about the gods’ view of love and virtue? And that in Hermesianax Agriope regained life is hardly under dispute, for all agree that she was alive when returned to Orpheus in the underworld; whether or not she made it out is not mentioned. And *Leontion*, in which the reference is found, is altogether about *tragic* loves, about the victims of overwhelming passion.

All of this is reinforced, Heath argues, by the fact that there’s much ancient pottery and art suggesting a link between Orpheus’s romance woes and his death by a group of women. But he is never depicted reunited with the resurrected Eurydice in the world of the living.

Merkley pushes back in his 2016 thesis on the *Gregorics*.[^12] Costabile, indeed, regards him as having decidedly “disproved” Heath in this thesis.[^13] Merkley argues that Admetus’s allusion to the Orpheus’s musical prowess can be understood ironically even if it is to a successful Orpheus; for example, he swears off the lyre and music entirely just a few lines prior. He also cites the fourth century BCE Palaephatus, who makes a seemingly unquestionable parallel between Hercules’s twelfth labor and Orpheus’s katabasis:

> As Herakles after going down into Hades led up Cerberus, so likewise Orpheus led up his wife.[^14]

None would doubt that Hercules suceeded in leading up the vicious dog. Thus, Merkley suggests, the other references are enormously bolstered; while they don’t go so far as to make explicit Orpheus’s success, surely they imply it given this explicit parallel. He goes on to suggest that perhaps the original contained only the first half of the quest: Orpheus receives Eurydice from the lords of the underworld, and the tale ends, implying some success. Various authors were then able to add their own addendum, as perhaps Plato did. He goes on to posit that the failure ending was invented not by Virgil but some earlier first century author.

From my limited and very amateur reading, I suppose that, even now, there remains no consensus with regards to this century-long debate—though “on all sides, various scholars claim the weight of consensus for their position.”[^15]

title: on the Remains of the Day

Some months after reading this book I encountered a fellow in a bookshop who exclaimed that it was a life wasted.[^1] But I don’t think this is at all the picture. The life is not over! No, “I gave my best to Lord Darlington. I gave him the very best I had to give, and now—well—I find I do not have a great deal more left to give,” he says, speaking in relation to his present service to Mr Farraday; but surely he is also speaking of how, in giving all of himself to that image of a butler, he had little left to give to the others in his life: his father, his friends, and Miss Kenton. At least Darlington “chose a certain path in life,” though it “proved to be a misguided one,” whereas he “can’t even say \[he\] made \[his\] own mistakes.” He gave his very best, yet it was a giving marked by a passivity which let moments and opportunities slip by. He thought himself to be carrying out the duties of his post in proper dignity, yet now he laments, “Really—one has to ask oneself—what dignity is there in that?”

But the man on the bench is unruffled. He responds that his attitude is all wrong, that he ought not to ponder so much on the past, for that should only put him in a deeper rut; but he should face what is ahead of him, to seize the “remains of the day.” And it appears that Stevens really takes this to heart. He suddenly thinks back to “bantering,” at which point one might suppose that he is only considering business again. But is he not really reflecting on the relational essence of such bantering, the “key to human warmth?” This, he realises, is what he has really lacked all this time. It is, perhaps, not the turning point for which one might hope or expect; instead we read something of a mellow resignation to that post which he has kept all these years—what else can be realistically done?—but with a brighter anticipation for the remaining years, and a newfound hope and will: a broader vision for what this post of his really is.

This second time through *The Lord of the Rings*, I have found myself caught up on a relatively obscure point. It comes on the fifth day after the attack on Weathertop, and as there is no way over the river except by the bridge, Strider remarks:

> “I am afraid we must go back to the Road here for a while. We have now come to the River Hoarwell, that the Elves call Mitheithel. It flows down out of the *Ettenmoors, the troll-fells* north of Rivendell, and joins the Loudwater away in the South. Some call it the Greyflood after that. It is a great water before it finds the Sea.”[^1]

Strider helpfully glosses the *Ettenmoors* as “troll-fells.” The more familiar component here is *moor*, which means a desolate, sometimes marshy and peaty land with poor soil, comparable to a bog, marsh, or fen. It corresponds to *fells*. Then *etten* must be related to *troll* in some way. The word derives from the Old English *[eoten]{lang="ang"}*, and it is commonly glossed into modern English as *giant*. Indeed, the trolls which Bilbo faces are quite large. But there is another word which often receives the same gloss: *ent*. This is a bewildering fact. For in Tolkienian usage the ents are the walking, talking shepherds of the trees, and beyond size they are most unlike the trolls. Over and against rash, crude, and wicked trolls, ents are measured, thoughtful, and principled. They are friends, not foes of the free peoples, and their dwindling is a terrible loss to creation. At risk of oversimplification, it is clear that, in the professor’s mind, *ettens* are bad and *ents* are good.

Nonetheless, they are both rendered “giant”, and even Tolkien was not free from such confusions, if only for a time. In the earliest map of Middle-earth for *The Lord of the Rings*, those lands north of Rivendell are first named the *Entish Lands*.[^2] They are renamed to the Trollfells, then Bergrisland, before the final Ettenmoors. The former survives as a gloss, but as for the latter, a *[bergrisi]{lang="non"}* is a mountain giant from Norse myth. The word *risi* appears quite synonymous with the perhaps better known *[jǫtunn]{lang="non"}*, whose kind inhabits *[Jǫtunheimr]{lang="non"}*. But the translation *giant* is quite problematic, as the *[jǫtnar]{lang="non"}* are not necessarily prodigious in size.

It might be said that the *[jǫtnar]{lang="non"}* are Norse cousins of the *[eotenas]{lang="ang"}*, for *[jǫtunn]{lang="non"}* and *[eoten]{lang="ang"}* are close cognates. And here, too, the gloss of *giant* obscures as much as it elucidates, for one finds that the Anglo-Saxon poetic mind recognises the *[eoten]{lang="ang"}* as a wondrously skilled blacksmith.[^3] We are told of how Beowulf wields the *[eald sweord eotenisc]{lang="ang"}*, the “old eotenish sword” taken from the cave of Grendel-dam (l. 1558). The phrase appears at least twice more in *Beowulf*, notably with Ongentheow’s helm cloven by the eotenish sword of Eofor thegn of Hygelac (l. 2979). Nonetheless, *[eotenas]{lang="ang"}* appear to be quite large; speaking of Beowulf’s *[eald sweord eotenisc]{lang="ang"}*, we are told that *[hit wæs mare ðonne ænig mon oð to beadulace ætberan meahte]{lang="ang"}*, that is, it was more than any other man might bear into battle (l. 1560–61). Interestingly, Mees notes a possible etymological association with gluttony, comparing Proto-Germanic *[\*etunaz]{lang="gem-x-proto"}*, which is linked with *[\*etanan]{lang="gem-x-proto"}*, “to eat”.[^4] One is reminded of the trolls’ fixation and prolonged discussion over their captured Dwarven meal.

Unlike in Old Icelandic, where *[jǫtunn]{lang="non"}* and *[þurs]{lang="non"}* appear mostly interchangeable, among the Anglo-Saxons *[eoten]{lang="ang"}* and *[þyrs]{lang="ang"}* seem to remain largely distinct categories. The *[þyrsa]{lang="ang"}* are the most negatively connoted, with common associations to sorcery and the demonic aspect of the supernatural. For instance, the glossary in the Brussels codex of Aldhelm’s *De Virginitate* gives *[þyrsa oððe wyrmgalera]{lang="ang"}*, or “*[þyrsa]{lang="ang"}* or snake charmers” for *[marsorum]{lang="la"}*, a Latin tribe renowned for witchcraft and the like. In his 19th century glossaries Thomas Wright associates the *[þyrs]{lang="ang"}* with the Latin *[orcus]{lang="la"}*, a personification of death; *[caci]{lang="la"}*, the fire-breathing giant who famously terrorised the Aventine Hill; and the *[colossi]{lang="la"}* and the *[cyclopes]{lang="la"}*. The most famous *[þyrs]{lang="ang"}* is Grendel, and his description is thoroughly monstrous, having scales and welts like steel. He is a *[sceadugenga]{lang="ang"}*, a “shadow walker.” But the boundaries of this ontology remain unclear. Grendel is also called an *[eoten]{lang="ang"}* and descended from Cain. The progeny of that first murderer are the *[eotenas]{lang="ang"}* and elves and orcs and giants—this time a loan from Latin *[gigantes]{lang="la"}*—and these are the enemies of God.

So also are the trolls in Tolkien, those corruptions of the Enemy. It is then no wonder that, no doubt thinking of Grendel, he should understood *etten* to mean something like a *troll*. But the helm of Ongentheow cloven by the *[eald sweord eotenisc]{lang="ang"}* is an *[entiscne helm]{lang="ang"}*. If *[eotenisc]{lang="ang"}* arms are so enormous, are also the armours of *entas* of an equal weight, and the man’s head so large to bear such a helm? No; but *[entas]{lang="ang"}* are quite different, and so are the Ents…

---
title: "#"
date: 2026-04-23
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings]
---
If the Anglo-Saxon word *ent* received a second life thanks to Tolkien, the \# has had so many that it is hard to pin down a particular number. Its many names—“number”, “hash”, “octothorpe”, “pound” among the prominent ones—attest to its scandalously diverse, context-dependent usage. Curiously, if only now known in North America by that last designation for its role in touch-tone dialing, the symbol appears to find its earliest origins in “pound”, that is, in the measure of weight.[^1]

For to the Romans twelve *uncia*, that is, *ounces*, made one *libra*, or pound, and these latter quantities they measured using *libra pondo*: pound weight. Our imperial systems have inherited this ancient term, such that even now we denote 5 pounds by writing 5 *lb*. The abbreviation entered English sometime in the fourteenth century, and scribal tradition employed the titulus-adorned ligature *℔*. Over time, haste and convenience seems to have reduced it to #.

But the old *libra pondo* #, if ever it possessed a distinct and cohesive identity, has been reappropriated across so wide a spread of domains as to suggest utter arbitrariness, complete disregard for any and all convention. It is variously a marker for ordinals, the notation for checkmate, a lazy typographical stand-in for ♯,[^2] or a proofreading mark. In three’s company it signals the end of a press release, ahead of a word it heralds a hashtag, or with its friend ! it becomes the shebang. One uses it to dial an extension or terminate an input, to signal the start of a code comment, a Markdown heading, or the URL fragment identifier. In the serendipity of historical whim it has been revived again and again by the capricious fancies of designers and inventors. It is something of an empty token, carrying no significance of its own but serving as a vessel to be filled and drained by the fiat of disparate semantic economies largely ignorant and indifferent to one another. But of course all symbols are like this, bearing no meaning but that which is given to them.

[^1]: Keith Houston, *Shady Characters: The Secret Life of Punctuation, Symbols & Other Typographical Marks*, First edition (New York, USA: W. W. Norton, 2013). [[houstonShadyCharactersSecret2013|q.v.]]

[^2]: The diesis or sharp symbol is distinct from #, having its origins in *b quadratum* ♮.

---
title: hwæt! or hwæt
date: 2026-01-20
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings, anglo-saxon, philology]
modified: 2026-08-03 19:20
---
::: {lang="ang"}
> Hwæt we Gardenum in geardagum\
> þeodcyninga þrym gefrunon\
> hu ða æþelingas ellen fremedon
:::

So *Beowulf* famously begins. The first word, *hwæt*, the interrogative pronoun meaning “what”, is most commonly rendered as an interjection, so that we have something like:

> Lo! We have heard of the might of the folk-kings in the bygone days of the Spear-Danes, how those princes performed deeds of valour.

Tolkien likewise renders it “Lo!”; in others we have “Listen!”, “So.”, or “Hark!”. In its entry for *what*, the *OED* remarks that *hwæt* may be “used to introduce or call attention to a statement” in older English. Apparently this interpretation of *[hwæt]{lang="ang"}* dates back even to Jacob Grimm, who concluded in 1837 that it is “purely an exclamation, albeit in a very moderate sense.” It possible that Shakespearean usage of “what!” as an interjection influenced this interpretation.

One can imagine, then, *[Hwaet!]{lang="ang"}* cutting through the chatter and merriment at a feast as the poet stands to tell his tale. Yet despite overwhelming scholarly consensus on this issue, some argue that this understanding is indefensible. As per Tolkien himself, “etymology is not a safe guide to sense.” Graham Scheper summarises the analysis of a paper by George Walkden.[^1]

- Ælfric, in his 10th century grammar of Latin, gives parallel lists of Latin and Old English interjections. The latter includes *[eala]{lang="ang"}* (oh!), *[wa is me]{lang="ang"}* (woe is me!), *[huig]{lang="ang"}* (alas!), *[wala]{lang="ang"}* (oh no!), *[afæstla]{lang="ang"}* (oh certainly), *[wellawell]{lang="ang"}* (well, well), and so on. Latin *[ecce]{lang="la"}* (see!, look!, behold!) is not glossed *[hwæt]{lang="ang"}* but *[efne nu]{lang="ang"}*. *[hwæt]{lang="ang"}* is absent from the list altogether. In over 800 occurrences of Latin *[ecce]{lang="la"}* in Old English texts, it is never glossed or translated *[hwæt]{lang="ang"}*.

- The overwhelming majority of root (or non-subordinate) clauses exhibit V1 (verb-first) or V2 (verb-second) word order (70% in Bede, 76% in Ælfric’s *Lives of Saints*). Sub-ordinate clauses are mostly V-later, where the verb is pushed after the object (62% and 39%). But *[hwæt]{lang="ang"}* also seems cause V-later word order in majority of cases, even in root clauses (69% and 56%). Notice how *Beowulf* does not begin *we gefrunon …* but *we … gefrunon*. This influence of *[hwæt]{lang="ang"}* on the grammatical structure of the rest of the clause indicates that it is not an interjection.

Rather, it seems that *[hwæt]{lang="ang"}* is more similar to how we might use “how” in introducing an exclamative: “How much you’ve grown!” Then the poet of *Beowulf* is really expressing an exclamation with regard to the *[þeodcyngina þrym]{lang="ang"}* and the *[ellen]{lang="ang"}* which the *[æþelingas fremedon]{lang="ang"}*:

> How we have heard of the might of the folk-kings in the bygone days of the Spear-Danes, how those princes performed deeds of valour.

*The Dream of the Rood*, which I read in sophomore year, begins similarly:

::: {lang="ang"}
> Hwæt ic swefna cyst secgan wille
:::

This is a root clause yet V-later: *[secgan]{lang="ang"}* (to speak) comes after *[swefna cyst]{lang="ang"}* (choicest dream). The poet does not order the listener to attention

> Listen! I will tell you of the choicest dream…

but rather boasts in the excellence of his dream and his narration.

> How I will tell you of the choicest dream…

[^1]: Graham Scheper, *The Most Horribly Mistranslated Word in Old English*, 2026; George Walkden, ‘The Status of Hwæt in Old English’, *English Language & Linguistics* 17, no. 3 (November 2013): 465–88, <https://doi.org/10.1017/S1360674313000129>. [[grahamscheperMostHorriblyMistranslated2026|q.v.]] [[walkdenStatusHwaetOld2013|q.v.]]

---
title: what does it take to go to bed?
date: 2026-01-26
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings]
---
Categories are helpful. There is *on* and *off*, *inside* and *outside*, *black* and *white*, *clean* and *unclean*. The last of these in particular I seem to have grasped onto more than others.

I don’t know when instinct became a kind of conscientious delineation, but certainly by high school I had developed a somewhat strict *spatial* sense of bodily *cleanness* and *uncleanness*. To go to bed, I had to be *clean*. The sole mechanism to become *clean* when I was *unclean* was, in my mind, to take a shower. What made one *unclean*? To come into contact with *unclean* objects, which included, for instance, anything outside the house, and perspiration. These axioms, of course, necessitated additional consideration; above all, they introduced notions of *inside* versus *outside* the house. Well, I seem to have subconsciously decided, the *inside* the house consisted also of the deck. There were also certain, hidden, temporal factors. After the body was *clean* for two days uninterrupted, it decayed into an unclean state. During the height of summer mere existence meant contact with perspiration.

The move into university dormitories complicated these rudimentary systems. Some refitting of existing principles meant that *inside* consisted of the entire dorm building. But there were certain complications. Whereas the previous two-valued logic reasoned in *cleanness* and *uncleanness*, I found that a third category existed in the wild: something closer to *foulness*. Indeed, the floor of the communal bathroom was unquestionably *foul*, and although_uncleanness\_ could be tolerated as long as it was cleansed before going to bed, contact with *foul* surfaces was to be altogether avoided. So I wore slippers to the bathroom.

But there is a problem: do not the slippers become *foul* by virtue of contact with the *foul* bathroom floor? At this point, it is interesting to note the mysteriously regulated *taint* property of *uncleanness*. An object is made *unclean* by way of contact with an already *unclean* object. This was true before: the body’s contact with *unclean* surfaces outside the home made it also *unclean*. But there seemed to be certain objects which resisted taint: somehow, an *unclean* body did not make a chair *unclean* such that a *clean* body sitting in it would become *unclean* again. Perhaps these may be taken as exceptions to the taint mechanism, though it is perhaps more elegant to understand that, even in the previous system, there were *already* three possible states: *clean*, *unclean*, and a sort of *neutral*; and *unclean* objects tainted only *clean* objects but not *neutral* ones. Thus furniture and floors in the home were inherently *neutral*. Now, if *uncleanness* is like its degenerate nephew *foulness* in this manner, we may understand slippers also to be *neutral*. With these middle-men between them and the *foul* bathroom floor, *clean* and *unclean* feet could remain as such.

But it is not quite correct to say that slippers are simply *neutral*; I regarded them as capable of transferring *foulness* from the bathroom to my room whilst leaving the body unaffected. Instead, we might do better to imagine a slipper, though *physically* a singular object, *metaphysically* as two conjoined objects: a bottom part and a *neutral* top part. *Neutrality* was, like *uncleanness*, a characteristic *inherent* to certain objects but, unlike *uncleanness*, did not taint.

This period saw an additional curious development. I often wore flip-flops during the warmer months, and my feet, being exposed to elements in a way they are not inside socks and shoes, picked up dirt and grime to a degree such that it would not do to consider the top part of a slipper as simply *neutral*. They became obviously *unclean* over time. Then, to wear them after showering would make the feet *unclean* again. I need *two* sets of slippers: one for *clean* feet and another for *unclean* feet. Each slipper was, then, three parts: a bottom part which was always *foul* (though not *inherently*), a top part which was either *clean* or *unclean*, and an intervening *neutral* layer.

There were times, of course, when I accidentally used the *unclean* slippers for *clean feet*, but it was sufficient to wash just the feet again. It seems that the body, previously taken as a whole, was refined into two parts separated by some *neutral* layer: the feet and the rest of the body. Washing made each clean, but the feet could become *unclean* without tainting the rest of the body.

But even this quaternary logic is deficient. I am prompted by the question: why are shoes worn outside not to be worn inside the house? Even supposing that they are *unclean* or even *foul*, in one way or another the supposed *neutrality* of the floor is not respected; somehow this makes the floor *unclean*, such that *clean* feet become *unclean* upon contact. One solution is to understand the bottom of shoes as *foul* and the floor as *unclean-resistant neutral* but not *foul-resistant neutral*. This is unsatisfying for at least two reasons. Firstly, the shoes do not make the floor *foul*—and therefore to be altogether avoided—but merely *unclean*. Secondly, the bottoms being not inherently *foul*, this suggests that the good, solid earth outside is like the floor of the university bathroom. The latter is rather objectionable where the former is not.

Then alongside *foulness* and *uncleanness* there is an additional category for which I struggle to find an appropriate name. It is like its cousins in that it taints, but it is unlike in that it does not respect *neutrality* and, when tainting, mellows out into *uncleanness*. But such a proliferation of additional categories with specialised taint behaviours is fraught with complexity: what happens when an object of this state contacts a *foul* object? Since *foulness* seems “worse”, surely it becomes *foul*. But does it then suddenly respect *neutrality*?

Indeed, notions of *neutrality* have severely complicated our understanding, breaking the simple ordering of *cleanness*, *uncleanness*, and *foulness*, where each may “decay” into the next via taint. Though intuitively somewhere between *cleanness* and *uncleanness* like a zero between positive one and negative one, from the perspective of *taint*, *neutrality* lies somewhere outside this ordering; it does not suffer taint and therefore does not decay, nor does it taint and cause decay.

Perhaps the simplest solution is to consider whether or not an object respects neutrality as a separate axis, say *respectfulness*. *Respectful* objects behave as previously described, but *disrespectful* objects override *neutrality* in taint. Additionally, just as *unclean* objects taint *clean* ones, *disrespectful* taint *respectful* ones. Better yet, the cleanliness categories themselves are qualified by *respectfulness*. The three basic cleanliness categories and two respectfulness categories give rise to six *qualified cleanliness* categories: *respectfully clean*, *disrespectfully clean*, *respectfully unclean*, *disrespectfully unclean*, *respectfully foul*, and *disrespectfully foul*.[^1] The taint behavior of *respectfulness* is carried over, such that the *disrespectful* categories may taint their *respectful* counterparts: for instance, *disrespectfully unclean* objects make *respectfully unclean* ones to become *disrespectfully unclean*.

Thus the ground outside is *disrespectfully unclean*, causing shoe bottoms to become the same. Wearing those shoes indoors causes the floor to also become *disrespectfully unclean*. There is a problem! Shouldn’t wearing slippers with clean feet on this sullied floor still keep the feet clean? But since the floor is *disrespectfully unclean*, the bottom of the slipper is likewise. Since it is *disrespectful*, the slipper’s neutral layer—and therefore the top—is *also* made *disrespectfully unclean*! This is severe indeed.

The situation is remedied by a distinction between *strong* and *weak neutralities*. The former is stubborn and unyielding even to disrespect, but the latter puts up little resistance. Hence the *weakly neutral* floor is easily tainted by *disrespectfully unclean* things, but the slipper’s middle is *strongly neutral*.[^2] It is pertinent, then, that the *weakly neutral* floor does not become *disrespectfully unclean* or *foul*. This rule, though only now properly formulated, has become so engrained as to provoke, at times, silent distress at the homes my friends, where shoes are worn about without much thought.

Indeed, these are the “policies” which denote the “bad situations.” That the bed must not become *unclean* was the original, explicit policy. Now, additional, implicit ones are coming into view. It is, in fact, a new policy which has in the first place prompted all this reflection.

I recently purchased a navy blue braided rug, which I quite like, and I find myself loath to step on it with my slippers, whose bottoms are rather blackened from soot (and categorically *unclean*). A new policy had already formed: the rug should remain *clean*. But it is rather inconvenient to take off one’s slippers before walking onto the rug and to need to return to that exact point on the perimeter to leave it. Instead, I supposed that I could make an effort to keep the entire living room *clean* and use slippers in only the hallway and the rest of the apartment. This is somewhat less error-prone, too, as the doorway is a signal to switch into and out of the slippers.

The astute reader will have noticed that this specific arrangement and policy are problematic for at least two reasons. Firstly, the rug policy is violated irrespective of slipper use: feet returning from the outside are *respectfully unclean*, and they taint the rug to become likewise *respectfully unclean*. Secondly, these issues cascade to the bed policy as well: *clean* feet which have stepped on the rug become *respectfully unclean* and therefore ineligible for entry into the bed. Does the problem go away if we consider the rug to be *weakly neutral*? Yes, but then one must wonder why *respectfully unclean* slipper bottoms should not be used upon it. It is insufficient to say that the rest of the apartment—and thus the slipper bottoms, too—are *disrespectfully unclean*, for then it would be permissible to walk around with shoes on. This situation calls for a richer set of *cleanliness* categories: there is some notion of *soiled* objects between *unclean* and *foul* ones. Even with this addition, the logic of *respectfulness* versus *weak* and *strong neutralities* extends in the obvious way. Therefore shoe bottoms are *disrespectfully soiled*, and floors may become *respectfully unclean* but not worse. The rest of the apartment and slipper bottoms are *disrespectfully unclean*, and the rug must remain *weakly neutral*.

After heaving a sigh of relief, one is confronted with the thought: what in the world have we spent the last 1900 words doing? There is a moment when the formalist must finally, amidst the flurry of definitions and axioms and corollaries, pause to wonder if his systems meaningfully reflect reality. Of course, there is at least *some*, if minimal, basis in an understanding of hygiene. But this is no logic for hygiene. I suppose it is rather a logic for the heart, which in the tumult of cares and worries latches onto firm guides. But few perhaps, if any, will sympathise fully with what has been unraveled here.

[^1]: In practice, of course, no objects are ever *disrespectfully clean*. Semantically, this category is nonsense, because *clean* objects should not taint neutral ones.

[^2]: In mathematical terms, we might have two sets partially ordered by decay: the *cleanliness* order *C* (*clean* \< *unclean* \< *foul* and an incomparable *neutral*) and the *strength* order *S* (*weak* \< *strong*). We take their coordinatewise product to construct the *qualified cleanliness* order *Q*. Of course, these correspond to *respectfully unclean*, *disrespectfully unclean*, and so on. Then, two objects with values *u* and *v* take on *u* ⊓ *v* upon contact. If there is no such least upper bound, at least one object is *weak* or *strong neutral*. In that case, we attempt again to take the least upper bound on a *neutrality respectfulness* order, where *weak neutral* \< *strong clean*, *strong unclean*, *strong foul*. Otherwise, the objects retain their original values.

I suppose it’s inevitable that a series like the Very Short Introductions should be rather hit-or-miss. Coming from Cunliffe’s introduction to the Celts, which I found to be very well-written, this volume on the Vikings was more difficult to get through.[^1] No doubt Richards possess a great amount of knowledge about the subject, but he’s perhaps not the right person to distill it into a short 140 page volume for beginners.

Agreeing with some other folks, it read much more like a survey of archaeological finds relating to the Vikings—Richards goes on for pages about different excavations and the objects found within them. Yet in most cases one doesn’t really come to understand which of those objects are significant and indicate a Scandinavian presence in that area, and what kind of continuity exists between Scandinavian expansion across different areas. There’s a noticeable lack of the kind of overarching discussion I enjoyed in *The Celts*: Richards occasionally makes the scant effort to summarize the finds, but these bits come off as half-effort transitions to the next chapters more than anything else. The result is that one doesn’t get much of a sense of who the Vikings were or in what their daily lives consisted, only with what they might have been buried. Nor, though he offhandedly mentions them a few times, does one come to understand how reality relates to the Scandinavian sagas.

The final two chapters were the strongest, I felt, in that I received something of an overall picture of the Greenland and North American colonies, and the discussion on how Viking identity has been altered and used in more recent times is exceptionally interesting.

As usual, the list of further readings is well appreciated—hopefully those may lead to some more focused introductions.

---
title: canon revisited
date: 2026-07-26
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings, theology, philosophy, epistemology]
modified: 2026-08-18 19:30
---
The so-called “‘Achilles’ heel’ of Protestant Christianity” is the problem of the canon: how can we know that we have the right books in the New Testament? If Christians, individually and corporately as the church, are called to dedicate their lives in their entirety to God and the gospel based on Bible, then it is certainly critical that those books which constitute that ultimate rule and authority actually be the inspired Word of God. But it seems that many things could go quite wrong. What if the books are not inspired, in whole or in part? What if there are inspired books missing from the canon? Even supposing that they *are* inspired, how could anyone *know* that they are? Is the Christian able to reasonably account for the origin and authority of his own canon?

The question addressed in Michael J. Kruger’s *Canon Revisited* is not a *de facto* one, that is, whether or not *Christian* belief in the authority and inspiration of the New Testament canon is *true* or *false* belief.[^1] Though this is important, the question at hand is a *de jure* one: whether or not Christians *can have a rational basis* for their thinking that they *know* the New Testament canon. A clarification is immediately necessary. The question is not about how a person comes to believe in the New Testament canon as Scripture (which may come about in many different ways), nor is it about how it may be “\[proven\] to the skeptic (in a way that would satisfy him) that these books are indeed from God.”[^2] Rather it is a question of epistemological accounting—even supposing that it happens to be a *true* belief, is *Christian* belief in the canon *justified*, or *warranted*? Here we say *Christian* with reference to a Christian worldview, not merely to say that the belief is a Christian one: can the Christian faith “give an adequate account for how it can be known that these books are canonical?”[^3]

The matter may seem like a purely historical or scientific one—indeed, the main of modern scholarship has taken a heavily (and thoroughly fallacious) evidentialist approach, focusing on dates and textual variations and historical causes. Above all one ought to be objective in his approach toward such matters, eschewing his own religious and philosophical propensities, to be an utterly neutral observer undertaking an utterly neutral investigation. Of course, this is itself a dogmatic position, and one which forgets the fact quite blindly. There is no religiously neutral approach to historical study, just as there is no religiously neutral approach to anything.[^4] The world is not divided into those who import religiosity into scholarship and those who remain somehow outside religion; it is divided into those who acknowledge the presence of theological presuppositions and confessional categories and those who do not.

It is not to say that historical characteristics have no bearing on the issue of canon; in fact, they are quite important. But history *alone* cannot answer the question. For historical positions do not merely turn on differences in data or technique but stem from prior and “broader methodological, epistemological, \[and\] theological” commitments, which in the context of the present question Kruger calls the *canonical model*.[^5] One’s canonical model gives rise to the process or system by which books are determined or *authenticated* to be canonical or non-canonical. Then, it should be unsurprising that to speak of the Christian religion’s ability to account for its canon—the *de jure* question—should concern the *Christian* canonical model and therefore include much talk about *Christian* understandings of God and history and revelation. It must concern the *Christian* understanding of knowledge, which, in the biblical view, is always with reference to and depends on God.[^6] *Canon Revisited* is therefore “unashamedly theological in its approach.”[^7]

---
title: the illusory middle landscape
date: 2025-01-10
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings]
modified: 2026-08-17 22:22
---
Plenty of people have spoken or written about the enduring mystic of *Skyrim*.[^1] In spite of newer experiences which may boast gripping narratives or real-to-life visuals or delightful gameplay loops, those whose hearts upon which has lain the tender yet unyielding hold of idyllic Tamriel find themselves returning again and again, even nigh fifteen years now, to that northern corner of the world. By most reasonable measures the game’s combat mechanics are rather lacking; the quest writing varies from uninspired to abominable; the whole experience is rife with visual and functional deficiency and defect, of both comic and exasperating character. Yet somehow it is a spring from which all weariness seems to depart; when novelties have worn off, one remembers the rugged peak and the rolling pastures, the surly wanderer and the unassuming stableboy, the hidden cove and the homely home.

At some point, I discovered mods, and then the possibilities had truly opened before me. For by way of the bottomless trove of fan-made modifications I could make the world a place of my own: new characters to befriend, equipment to craft; fair vistas to behold, deep dungeons to crawl. In short time I discovered, somewhat paradoxically, that I could only best feed my wants and my wonders by imposing restrictions. It is absurd, after all, to wade an icy bay or eat a half-quiver of arrows without much thought; one should suffer, respectively, hypothermia and outright death. One should wonder why prisoners remain in their chains and carriage drivers at their seats if instantaneous teleportation to any point-of-interest costs neither money nor effort. Until then I had cared for good cooking only after a brutal stabbing. Now I respected the snow squalls and esteemed the bow; I cherished my feet and my mare; I welcomed to my parched lips even the most insipid soup and stale loaf after any jaunt in the woods. For the harsh land of the Nords demands hardy living; by endurance one claims the high ridge; through peril comes the richest spoil; it is hinter that makes home. The untamed grasses rush furiously, I can see the glowing parapet, nigh is the slumbering grove; here I stand. Its name is Wilderness—yet not altogether Wild.

This was the reality away from reality: the middle landscape, fraught with many dangers—but comforts are never so out of reach. I sought more, driven to wilder wilds, homelier homes. But satisfaction is a doomed pursuit, a chasing after the wind. There is always some greater adventure to be tasted. It is this regrettable truth that, whilst no longer surprising, never loses its vexing flavour. For the sub-creation of man, though it may refract and magnify the splintered rays of the primary creation, remains just that: a partial mosaic of the hale light. And man whose heart was made for the light has fallen very far into the shadows.

title: fires pro/escribed
date: 2026-05-29
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings]

At the southern edge of Yosemite National Park, Mariposa Grove is home to many giant sequoias, among these the so-called Fallen Monarch and Grizzly Giant. Their names speak for themselves. Between these majestic lords stand many princes of their kin, and together they tower over the firs, pines, and cedars; and lupines, wild strawberries, willows, and dogwoods occupy the understory. But there are also many blackened stumps and stakes. Dead trees dot the landscape, and the effects of fire follow the trail.

That so many of the wayside exhibits focus on the role of fire in this ecosystem suggests that our natural inclinations have not changed much from colonial settlers of the nineteenth century who felt the need to put out all fires, even natural ones. There is a rather striking exhibit which shows three photographs of the same spot in the Upper Grove in the years 1890, 1970, and 2000. The giant sequoias which naturally dominate in the first are crowded out by other conifers in the second. Whereas periodic fires would have cleared out the understory, dried out sequoia cones to loosen their seeds, and helped with germination, a hundred years of suppression favoured smaller, more shade-tolerant vegetation. Since realising this, the National Park Service has conducted prescribed burns to pattern natural fires. Thirty years later, sequoias once again command the landscape.

No doubt these prescribed burns are quite controlled, and I should guess that certain spots—the ones for which visitors like myself come—are carefully managed. Far from allowing nature to run its course, we have sought to mimic its ways such that what we wish to see remains there. The natural fires are still suppressed. Humans still dominate Mariposa Grove, controlling it even more tightly than before. If John Muir’s “wilderness” tradition which established Yosemite values nature as something untouched and undegraded by human presence, it has ironically collapsed on itself. Restoration is domination. Such a dichotomy between nature and civilisation fails, anyway, to do justice to history. Pristine, untouched nature is an artifact of a Western or peculiarly American cultural imagination. But Sierra Nevada and other regions have been shaped and managed by indigenous peoples for millennia. To speak of the “natural,” one is left wondering, *which* one? Pre-industrial, pre-Columbian, pre-agricultural? When did nature become tainted?

The conservationist who denies a simplistic nature-culture dualism must appeal to alternative grounds, whether biodiversity or the intrinsic value of non-human life; or more anthropocentric values such as sustained ecological benefit or simply beauty. All of these require judgments of value, and they surely lead to different outcomes. As for me, I am interested in developing a well-grounded, *theocentric* understanding, of this extraordinarily beautiful creation and our role within it.

title: streams in the delta
tags: [musings, theology, philosophy]
modified: 2026-08-06 18:43

date: 2026-04-17

From a friend of a friend I was recently introduced to the idea that Time might be conceived of as a delta. Our individual streams flow, sometimes slowly and sometimes quickly, winding. We cross with the streams of those around us, at times touching, mingling, exchanging; others we pass by, never to wander near again. We meet, and we depart. We experience, and remember. Even if we forget, that does not change the fact that it happened. If nothing else there is progress, that continuum of irreversible change.

That change and not substance is the chief observable data of the universe seems to lie at the core of such process philosophies. These I know little about, and process theology I have only interacted with briefly and on occasion. The latter seems objectionable on both [[49yo|epistemological]] and biblical grounds, but I can, of course, see the appeal. If God not only affects but also is affected by temporal processes, then God is dynamic and responsive. Not “is” but “becoming.” God does not rule with a rigid and coercive, deterministic fiat; being the “fellow sufferer who understands” he persuades with love. The creaturely will is respected, and evil is not an inexplicable failure in a God’s plan but the consequence of genuine freedom.

The objections are many. Such a God is metaphysically weak; too weak to exist in himself, to create properly or know exhaustively, to ground any normativity, or to provide eschatological hope. The God of Whitehead and other process philosophers seems to trade divine simplicity and glory for modern intelligibility. But specific objections aside, it seems to me that classical theism has all the resources to understand God as impassible and omnipotent and yet—or, indeed—dynamic and responsive. God’s eternal, living existence is the infinite act of triune love. In this God is unchanging in his infinite wisdom and compassion. He is never static and cold but always dynamic and overflowing with love, and all this is exhibited in his creation and providence. And God is responsive: he makes promises and binds himself to them; he remembers and forgets; he rejoices and regrets. These are not mere anthropomorphisms to be explained away with a nervous chuckle. But in his maximal simplicity and utter freedom he wills and takes on such change of his own accord. He does not undergo but enters into change; the source of all change is the Creator, not his creatures.

And these truths are exhibited most gloriously in Incarnation. Looking toward the joy set before him, Christ Jesus emptied himself of his own accord. For the Son freely took upon himself human nature, a mode of existence characterized by genuine temporality, real “becoming,” undergoing true occasions. In the world he created, God became man and experienced joy, surprise, and pain, worry, and grief as we do. And these he conquered for our sake. Then in this ultimate, free kenosis the divine stream pours out across the delta, bursting forth in the spotless lucidity, vibrant and full of life. It cuts its way through our own streams yet remains unpolluted by their foulness. But abundantly and eagerly it purifies and gives us new life.

title: externally authenticated canonical models
date: 2026-07-30
author: Eric Zhao
tags: [musings, theology, philosophy, epistemology]

*Canon Revisited* [[vgm5|seeks to address]] the *de jure* objection to Christian belief in the inspiration and authority of the New Testament canon: even supposing that the belief is a *true* belief, Christians have no way of *knowing* that it is true.[^1] In other words, Christians are not *warranted* in holding the belief rationally, since there is no justification for it. Though the question may seem alarmingly circular—if one assumes that Scripture is inspired and authoritative, why is there need to justify it?—but that a statement is *true* is distinct from one’s *knowing* that it is true. It may be raining outside, but if I am in a deep underground cell where I cannot see the rain, hear the drops, nor feel the moisture, then it is another matter to say that I *know* it is raining. I may sincerely *believe* that it is raining (because I believe that my sneezing three times within a minute usually means it is raining), and I happen to be right in that moment, but arguably I cannot be said to *know* the fact. Truth does not necessarily imply *epistemic access*.

How then can belief in the twenty-seven New Testament books as the ultimate rule and authority be justified? Kruger undertakes this question through the examination of *canonical models*. A model encapsulates the methodological, philosophical, and theological commitments which ultimately drive the process by which Scripture and the canon are authenticated—and determine whether or not the canon thus formed can properly function as the Christian’s rule. Naturally, then, the Christian canonical model begins with the inspiration and authority of Scripture itself, and the task is to show that Scripture “testifies to the fact that God has created the proper epistemic environment wherein belief in the New Testament canon can be reliably formed,” and that such a canon can function as the church’s ultimate rule and authority.[^2] In this way, the Scripture is *self-authenticating*.

But in the initial two chapters, Kruger first considers a variety of canonical models—candidate models, one might say—whose characteristics will inform the self-authenticating model. They are divided roughly into two broad categories. *Community-determined* canonical models understand the canon as something “established or constituted by the people—either individually or corporately—who have received these books as Scripture.”[^3] The New Testament books are canonical not because of intrinsic qualities of the text but because the community has *responded* and ascribed to it such status. These models may appear quite different yet share this fundamental characteristic. Of course, the historical-critical model prevalent in the modern academy is one example, and it views the development of the canon (as all historical developments) as something of an accident in which so-called orthodox strains within the early church won out against their rivals, whether due to ordinary chance or political jockeying. The New Testament canon is the church’s *self-description*, perhaps especially against the heterodox. Needless to say, that the canon is *entirely* human-conditioned and devoid of divine involvement, though something that higher critics regularly attempt to prove, is the consequence of the historical-critical model’s precommitment to philosophical naturalism. This commitment is [[49yo|epistemologically precarious]], though we will not discuss this now. Such naturalistic presuppositions are readily rejected by other canonical models, yet the canon’s fundamental determination by community response is shared, Kruger argues, by such disparate approaches as the Roman Catholic dogma (canon as decreed by the church), the so-called canonical-criticism (canon as the final redaction of the text), and the existentialism-neoorthodoxy of Barthian tradition (canon as defined by subjective encounter). Though we will not consider any of these deeply here, it can be said that these models rightly recognise the role of the churches in the development of the canon but make the mistake of *absolutising* the ecclesial aspect such that “it becomes the defining characteristic of canon.”[^4] A canon which is *derived and established by* the church is unable to *rule over* the church. Of course, to the higher critic this is typically no problem at all—he is quite happy to “deconstruct” the canon entirely—but for the Christian “the canon has so much become the church’s book that it is unable to be God’s book.”[^5]

Whereas community-determined models focus on the reception of the New Testament books, *historically determined* models seek to establish the canon by investigation of their historical *origins*. One manifestation of this approach is the canon-within-the-canon model. The canon clearly includes much problematic material, said Kurt Aland in 1962, hence one must unearth the “canon within the canon” containing the genuine “core.”[^6] The methodologies are typically shared with historical-critical scholarship, and the search for the “historical” Jesus has all but consumed the post-Enlightenment academy. Besides the embarrassingly diverse and contradictory conclusions of higher-critical methodologies, philosophical and theological convictions inevitably creep back in. The canon is subjected to some standard chosen by the scholar—whether or not it teaches, say, “moral improvement,” or an egalitarian agenda, or the liberation of the oppressed; or even, as Luther famously wrote, “whatever preaches Christ.”[^7] The key question seldom answered is where that standard comes from and how it may be known, beyond the redactor’s private motives and concerns. Even J.D. Crossan, a founding member of the Jesus Seminar, laments that “it is impossible to avoid the suspicion that historical Jesus research is a very safe place to do theology and call it history, to do autobiography and call it biography.”[^8] The criteria-of-canonicity model encounters a similar issue. It appeals to criteria—orthodoxy, usage, and, principally, *apostolicity*—to demonstrate, via neutral historical investigation, the truth of the canon. We have already noted that, though Habermas should like to suggest that “we just must be careful not to read biases into the \[historical\] accounts,” neutral historical reconstruction is a Enlightenment illusion; nor does the Bible speak of so-called neutrality as a virtue.[^9] Additionally, whence these criteria? Again, it is not to say that the historicity and apostolicity of the New Testament books are not important—indeed, the model helpfully identifies these aspects as data of significance—but historical evidence cannot stand alone; it is not self-interpreting. This is the problem shared by all such *externally determined* canonical models: the canon is subjected to standards and measures external to Scripture itself, destroying its capacity to function as the ultimate rule *over* our lives, including our standards and measures. As much as the authority of the canon is dependent upon the church in community-determined models, in historically determined models it is contingent on the subjective and fragile accords of historical methodology. Hence “the canon ceases to be a norm that *guides* our historical investigations, but becomes merely the *product* of our historical investigations.”[^10]

The flaw of these models is that “they all ground the authority of the canon in something outside the canon itself.”[^11] A canon which supposedly bears the very of authority of God, who is above all and “\[swears\] by himself”, cannot be grounded in human judgment or other lower authorities.[^12] “If we try to validate an ultimate authority by appealing to some other authority, then we have just shown that it is not really the ultimate authority.”[^13] This was the teaching of the Reformers and the foundation for *sola scriptura*: the canon must be *self-authenticating*; God himself bears witness to the authority of his Word. To answer the question of the canon then, one must engage in *biblical* theology. What does the Bible say about itself and how it ought to be apprehended? This does not mean that the extrabiblical data of historical study ought to be wholly diminished or ignored. They are indeed *essential*. Only, they are not *independent* of Scripture but “should always be seen as something *warranted by* Scripture and *interpreted by* Scripture”.[^14] Indeed, the self-authenticating canon testifies to its divine origins and authority through internal and external evidence, providentially received by the church and interpreted by the internal testimony of the Holy Spirit.

date: 2026-05-03

---
> Here we come a-wassailing\
> Among the leaves so green;\
> here we come a-wand’ring\
> so fair to be seen.

The classic Christmas carol begins with the word *wassail*, prefixed by the archaic intensifier *a-*. Now it means to carouse and revel by going from house to house singing carols, but this sense seems to have derived from and displaced an earlier one: “Wassail,” one would proclaim with a toast, especially in Twelfth night and Christmas eve celebration. In Layamon’s *Brut*, the knight Keredic explains to Vortigern, king of Britons: [^1]

::: {lang="ang"}
> Hit beoð tiðende; inne Sæxe-londe.\
> whær-swa æi duȝeðe gladieð of drenche;\
> þat freond sæiðe to freonde; mid fæire loten hende.\
> Leofue freond wæs hail; Þe oðer sæið Drinc hail.
:::

> It is the custom in Saxon lands\
> whenever a company is glad of drink,\
> that friend says to friend, with courteous gesture,\
> “Dear friend, wassail.” The other says, “Drink-hail.”

“[Wæs hail]{lang="ang"}”, one says, that is, “Be well!” Before it was enshrined in drinking formulas it was an ordinary salutation from Old English times. Here *[wæs]{lang="ang"}* is the singular imperative of *[wesan]{lang="ang"}* (“to be”), and *[hail]{lang="ang"}* comes from Old Norse *[heil]{lang="non"}*, synonymous with Old English *[hāl]{lang="ang"}* (“hale, whole”). Thus, Éomer, overjoyed at the return of Théoden his king into his own, cries, “*Westu Théoden hál!*.”[^2] It is one of Tolkien’s many linguistic jokes: Rohirric is only the Anglo-Saxon of the West Midlands, where Tolkien himself grew up.[^3] *[Westu]{lang="ang"}* is a dialectical variant of *[wæs þu]{lang="ang"}*: “Be thou, Théoden, well!” Later, as the Riders prepare to marshal forth against Isengard, Éowyn brings wine to the king, saying, “*Ferthu Théoden hál!*” Here *ferthu* is surely *[fer þu]{lang="ang"}*, again a singular imperative, for *[feran]{lang="ang"}*, “to go” or “set out.”

The chapter is filled with many a delightful homage to ancient England, and the Rohirrim are, as many have noted before, a calque on the Anglo-Saxons. Naturally, Tolkien’s feeling for those older days can be gleaned in part from that which Legolas says about their language. “‘That, I guess, is the language of the Rohirrim,’ said Legolas; ‘for it is like to this land itself; rich and rolling in part, and else hard and stern as the mountains. But I cannot guess what it means, save that it is laden with the sadness of Mortal Men.’” In that “slow tongue unknown” to the others no doubt Aragorn had chanted, *[hwær cwom mearg; hwær cwom mago?]{lang="ang"}*, those words from *The Wanderer* taken for Eorl: “*Where now the horse and the rider?*”

If the Rohirrim are the Old English, then hobbits are the English. In this way they are closer to one another than to others. And in characteristic Tolkien fashion, this connection is made fast by linguistic tie. Upon meeting Merry and Pippin at Isengard, the king remarks:

> ‘Are not these the Halflings, that some among us call the Holbytlan?’
>
> ‘Hobbits, if you please, lord,’ said Pippin.
>
> ‘Hobbits’? said Théoden. ‘Your tongue is strangely changed.’[^4]

Strangely changed indeed! The exchange harkens back to the very first line of *The Hobbit*, written nearly twenty years prior: “In a hole in the ground there lived a hobbit.”[^5] For *[holbytlan]{lang="ang"}* would be the plural of a weakly declined *[\*holbytla]{lang="ang"}*, that is, “hole-builder.”
